## 3. Textbook ML vs finance ML

**Minutes 3 to 6 (3 min).**

Machine learning, ML for short, means a computer learning a rule from examples instead of being told the rule. Most courses teach it on textbook problems such as recognising cats in photos, and finance breaks almost every assumption those problems make, row by row. Data: in a textbook each example is a fresh, independent draw from one process that never changes; market data arrives in time order, and the process itself changes, which is called a change of regime, for example calm years followed by a crisis. And companies that fail tend to drop out of the data, so what is left looks better than the past really was; that is survivorship bias, slide 21. Signal: a photo of a cat really does show a cat, but a stock's past gives only a tiny hint about its future, buried in random day to day noise. Validation means testing a model fairly on data it did not learn from. Textbooks shuffle the rows and cut them into k groups, testing on each group in turn, which is called k-fold cross-validation; in finance we test only on later years, called walk-forward, or we remove the training rows that overlap the test, called purging, and leave a gap. Labels are the answers we train on. Ours are five-day returns, so the labels of neighbouring days share four of their five days, and that overlap is why the sample size row says there are fewer independent observations than the row count suggests. Models: regularised means a model is held back from fitting too closely, and a tree ensemble is many decision trees averaged, which we build later. On tables of stock characteristics, Gu, Kelly and Xiu found that trees and neural networks beat linear models, and that among neural networks a few hidden layers, best at three, beat deeper ones. Deep learning is used mainly on raw order-book data, the live list of orders waiting to buy and sell, as in Sirignano and Cont. Text models are often simple, as in Ke, Kelly and Xiu, who use a screening and topic model rather than a deep network. Features are the inputs: textbook models often read raw inputs such as pixels or words and learn their own features end to end, while finance features are engineered by hand from prices, company accounts and other data. Success: a textbook scores accuracy, or a loss, on held-out data. In finance the first question is whether the forecasts rank the stocks correctly on data the model never saw, measured by the information coefficient, IC, the correlation between forecasts and outcomes across stocks on one day; then a trader scores money made per unit of risk after paying for every trade, the Sharpe ratio after costs, which slide 19 defines. López de Prado lists ten common mistakes in financial machine learning; today we meet two of them, leakage and backtest overfitting, and the noise in the signal row is why they bite. From now on, a small blue tag at the top right of a slide, In finance, names the row of this table that the slide is about.

## 7. The feature universe

**Minutes 14 to 18 (4 min).**

A feature is any number we know on day t that might hint at what a stock does next, and researchers have proposed hundreds of them. Here they are in eight families, each with the data it needs. Price and technical features come from the price alone: momentum, the recent trend; short-term reversal, recent winners slipping back; and how far the price sits below its highest point of the past year, the 52-week high. Volatility and risk: realised volatility is how much the price actually swung; beta is how strongly a stock moves with the whole market; idiosyncratic volatility is the part of its swings the market does not explain. Volume and liquidity: a volume surprise is trading far above or below the usual level; turnover is shares traded as a share of all shares; the bid-ask spread is the gap between the price you can buy at and the price you can sell at; Amihud illiquidity measures how far the price moves for each dollar traded. Fundamentals come from company accounts. Value compares the accounting worth of a company with its market price, for example book-to-market, which Fama and French built into their famous risk factors; quality asks how profitable the company is and how much of its profit is real cash rather than accruals, which are accounting entries not yet paid in cash; investment asks how fast the company is growing its assets. Analysts and events: an earnings surprise is reported profit minus what analysts expected, and Bernard and Thomas documented that prices keep drifting in the direction of the surprise after the announcement, called post-earnings-announcement drift; revisions are analysts changing their forecasts. Alternative data is anything new and unusual: the text of news and company filings, website visits, card spending, even satellite pictures of car parks. The order book is the live list of orders waiting to buy and sell, and imbalance means more waiting on one side. Macro and cross-asset features describe the whole economy: interest rates, credit spreads, which are the extra interest risky companies pay, and the VIX, a measure of expected market volatility taken from option prices. A warning about the universe: more features is not more signal. Green, Hand and Zhang tested 94 characteristics and found only 12 gave independent information among stocks that are not microcaps, the tiniest companies, from 1980 to 2014, and just two since 2003. Of 452 published anomalies, 65% fail the usual t of 1.96 once microcaps are down-weighted (Hou, Xue and Zhang 2020). Our notebook draws on only the three marked families, and only on the members named on each card: past returns and the 52-week high, 21 and 63-day volatility, and the volume surprise. Beta, spreads, Amihud illiquidity and everything else on the slide are not in it, because they need data we do not have for free. The list is not exhaustive either: options-implied measures, short interest, who owns the shares and what insiders trade, calendar seasonality and ESG scores, ratings of environmental, social and governance behaviour, are all used too.

Full citation: Green, J., Hand, J. R. M. and Zhang, X. F. (2017). "The Characteristics that Provide Independent Information about Average U.S. Monthly Stock Returns." The Review of Financial Studies, 30(12), 4389-4436.

Full citation: Hou, K., Xue, C. and Zhang, L. (2020). "Replicating Anomalies." The Review of Financial Studies, 33(5), 2019-2133.

## 8. Momentum and reversal, from the literature

**Minutes 18 to 21 (3 min).**

Two of the oldest features in the universe come from price alone, and they come from decades of research rather than from today. Jegadeesh and Titman found that buying past winners and selling past losers paid off over three to twelve month horizons in historical US data: that is called momentum. Jegadeesh found the opposite at the one month scale: last month's winners tended to fall back, which is called reversal. In our notebook, ret_63, the return over the past 63 trading days or about three months, sits at the short end of the momentum range, while ret_1 and ret_5, the past day and week, are short-horizon returns at the scale where reversal would show. Your Task 1 lookback replaces the 21-day return in the feature list, so ret_21 is there only if you keep the lookback at 21: a lookback of 63 to 252 days leans towards momentum, 21 or fewer towards reversal. Whether these effects survive in our 40 stocks and our years is exactly what the notebook tests; a published result is a reason to try a feature, not a promise.

Reference: Jegadeesh & Titman (1993), Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers, J. Finance 48(1), 65-91; Jegadeesh (1990), Evidence of Predictable Behavior of Security Returns, J. Finance 45(3), 881-898

Full citation: Jegadeesh, N. and Titman, S. (1993). "Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers: Implications for Stock Market Efficiency." The Journal of Finance, 48(1), 65-91.

Full citation: Jegadeesh, N. (1990). "Evidence of Predictable Behavior of Security Returns." The Journal of Finance, 45(3), 881-898.

## 10. Making features usable

**Minutes 24 to 27 (3 min).**

Raw features are awkward for a model: in a calm year every return is small, in a crisis every return is large, so the same raw number means different things in different years. What our notebook does is rank: on each date, each feature is replaced by its position among the 40 stocks, scaled so the middle stock is zero. The chart shows one date, 10 October 2008, in the week of the crash: on the left, the raw 21-day return, where 39 of the 40 stocks were down and the average was minus 24.9 percent; on the right, the rank, which still spreads evenly from about minus a half to plus a half. The order of the stocks is kept and the scale is thrown away, so the model sees which stocks did better than the others, which is what a long/short book trades. A z-score does something similar while keeping distances: subtract the day's average and divide by the day's spread, its standard deviation. Winsorising caps the most extreme values, so one wild stock cannot dominate. An interaction combines two features, for example momentum multiplied by volatility, for a model that cannot combine them by itself. Fractional differencing, from López de Prado's book, is a way to make a price series stable, so its behaviour does not drift over time, while keeping more of its memory than plain returns do. And one warning for anyone who goes on to use company accounts: companies sometimes correct, or restate, their numbers months later, and a database may show only the corrected figure. Using it on the original date leaks information nobody had then. Data stored as it was known on each date is called point-in-time data. That is leakage, which is the next slide.

Reference: López de Prado (2018), Advances in Financial Machine Learning, Wiley, ch. 5, Fractionally Differentiated Features

Full citation: López de Prado, M. (2018). Advances in Financial Machine Learning. Wiley. Chapter 5 "Fractionally Differentiated Features".

## 13. Split by time, never at random

**Minutes 33 to 37 (4 min).** ▶ Notebook: run section 3, *A baseline and a fair test*.

To test a model fairly, we hide some data from it, train on the rest, then check it on the hidden part. In most machine learning courses you shuffle rows at random, but in finance that leaks: neighbouring days are related, and a shuffled training set contains days just after a test day. López de Prado explains why ordinary shuffled cross-validation fails on financial data. So we split by time, train first, then a small gap, then test, and the gap stops five-day targets overlapping the boundary. Task 3 in section 3: choose your own two dates. ## 19. Trying many things is the problem

**Minutes 52 to 54 (2 min).**

The Sharpe ratio is the standard score for a strategy: average yearly return divided by how much it swings, so higher is better. Bailey and colleagues show that if you try enough variations, one will look great by luck alone. With a two-year backtest, about seven tries of strategies with no skill at all is enough to expect one with a Sharpe ratio around one. The curve is that calculation: it keeps rising as you try more, with zero real skill. This is why you should count your tries and touch the test set once. The In finance tag says Success: in finance success is scored with numbers like the Sharpe ratio, and those are exactly the numbers that luck inflates when you try many things.

Reference: Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado, Zhu (2014), Pseudo-Mathematics and Financial Charlatanism, Notices of the AMS 61(5), 458-471

Full citation: Bailey, D. H., Borwein, J. M., López de Prado, M. and Zhu, Q. J. (2014). "Pseudo-Mathematics and Financial Charlatanism: The Effects of Backtest Overfitting on Out-of-Sample Performance." Notices of the AMS, 61(5), 458-471. (open access at ams.org)

## 20. Forecast to portfolio, costs

## 21. Survivorship

**Minutes 58 to 60 (2 min).**

Here is the honest flaw in our data. We picked companies that still trade today, so every company that went bust or was taken over between 2000 and 2019 is missing. An investor in 2000 could not have known which companies would survive, so our backtest gets a hint from the future. Shumway showed that even a major research database was missing many delisting returns, so this is a real problem, not a classroom one. The In finance tag says Data: what is missing from a dataset can matter as much as what is in it.

## 25. The one question

**Bonus minutes 1 to 2 (1 min).**

All of learning theory comes down to one question: will this rule work on data it has never seen? That ability is called generalisation. Training error is how wrong the model is on the examples it learned from; test error is how wrong it is on new examples, and only the test error tells you what will happen next. Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman open their chapter on model assessment with exactly this distinction, and show that training error keeps falling as a model gets more flexible while test error does not. You saw it in Part 1: the unbraked forest scored about 48 to 60 percent R squared on its training rows and below zero on the test years. The picture on the right is that experiment again.

Reference: Hastie, Tibshirani, Friedman (2009), The Elements of Statistical Learning, 2nd ed., Springer, ch. 7, Model Assessment and Selection

## 26. What every model assumes

**Bonus minutes 2 to 3 (1 min).**

Behind every test score sits one assumption: the new data comes from the same process as the old data. Statisticians say the examples are drawn from the same distribution, which just means the same random mechanism produces them. If that holds, the error on a fair test set is a good estimate of the error you will see tomorrow; the theory in Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman's chapter 7 rests on it. Markets break it. The rules change, the people trading change, and when a pattern becomes well known, traders crowd into it and it can fade. A lasting change in how the market behaves is called a regime change. This is the data row of the table on slide 3, and it is why testing on later years matters. López de Prado warns that a test drawn from the same years cannot reveal a change of regime, and he recommends purged cross-validation over a single walk-forward path.

Reference: Hastie et al. (2009), The Elements of Statistical Learning, ch. 7; López de Prado (2018), J. Portfolio Management 44(6), 120-133

Full citation: Hastie, T., Tibshirani, R. and Friedman, J. (2009). The Elements of Statistical Learning (2nd ed.). Springer. Chapter 7 "Model Assessment and Selection".

Full citation: López de Prado, M. (2018). "The 10 Reasons Most Machine Learning Funds Fail." The Journal of Portfolio Management, 44(6), 120-133.

## 27. Train, validation, test

**Bonus minutes 3 to 4 (1 min).**

An honest workflow splits the data three ways, each with one job. The training set is for fitting: the model learns its parameters there, the numbers it adjusts itself, such as where each tree splits. The validation set is for choosing: settings that we pick rather than the model, called hyperparameters, such as the depth of a tree, are compared on it, and the best one is kept. The test set is for judging: the final choice is scored on it once, and that number is the one you report. In finance the three sets follow time order, oldest first, with gaps between them. If you look at the test score and then change something, the test set has started doing the validation set's job, and its score is no longer an honest estimate. Part 1 used only a training and a test period to keep things short, which means every knob you tuned while watching the test years spent a little of their honesty.

Reference: Hastie et al. (2009), The Elements of Statistical Learning, ch. 7, Model Assessment and Selection

Full citation: Hastie, T., Tibshirani, R. and Friedman, J. (2009). The Elements of Statistical Learning (2nd ed.). Springer. Chapter 7 "Model Assessment and Selection".

## 28. Cross-validation

**Bonus minutes 4 to 6 (2 min).**

One validation set gives one noisy score. Cross-validation, formalised by Stone in 1974, reuses the data: cut it into k pieces called folds, train on all but one, test on the one left out, repeat until every fold has been the test once, and average the scores. The top row of the picture is the textbook version, with the rows shuffled first. That is fine when every row is independent, but in finance a test day sits right next to training days whose five-day labels overlap its own, so the model has effectively seen part of the answer. That is the random split from Part 1, which looked five times better on R squared, plus 0.56 percent against plus 0.11 percent, and nearly twice as good on the daily IC, 0.0201 against 0.0123, for exactly this reason. The middle row is walk-forward: the training window always ends before the test block begins, and grows as we move through time. The bottom row is López de Prado's purged k-fold. Purging removes training rows whose label period overlaps the test fold, and the embargo removes a few more rows just after the test fold, because information can leak forwards too. This lets every year take a turn as the test while keeping the test honest. Part 2 builds purged cross-validation in code.

Reference: Stone (1974), Cross-Validatory Choice and Assessment of Statistical Predictions, J. Royal Statistical Society B 36(2), 111-147; López de Prado (2018), Advances in Financial Machine Learning, ch. 7

Full citation: Stone, M. (1974). "Cross-Validatory Choice and Assessment of Statistical Predictions." Journal of the Royal Statistical Society: Series B, 36(2), 111-147.

Full citation: López de Prado, M. (2018). Advances in Financial Machine Learning. Wiley. Chapter 7 "Cross-Validation in Finance".

## 29. The test set is spent once

**Bonus minutes 6 to 7 (1 min).**

A test set works like an exam paper: it measures you fairly only if you have not seen it. Data snooping is reusing the same data again and again to choose among ideas; each idea may be tested honestly, but the winner of the contest is flattered by luck. White's reality check is a statistical test built for this: instead of asking whether your final strategy beats a benchmark, it asks whether the best of all the strategies you tried does, which is a much harder bar. This is slide 19 from Part 1 again, where about seven useless strategies were enough to expect one with a Sharpe ratio near one by luck. The practical rule: keep a final period of data locked away, open it once at the very end, and write down how many things you tried before that.

Reference: White (2000), A Reality Check for Data Snooping, Econometrica 68(5), 1097-1126

Full citation: White, H. (2000). "A Reality Check for Data Snooping." Econometrica, 68(5), 1097-1126.

## 30. Bias and variance

**Bonus minutes 7 to 9 (2 min).**

Imagine training the same kind of model many times, each time on a fresh sample of training data, and throwing darts at a target with each fitted model. That is what the four dartboards show. Bias is how far the centre of the darts is from the bullseye: the model is wrong on average, usually because it is too simple, like a straight line fitted to a curve. Variance is how scattered the darts are: the model changes a lot depending on which training sample it happened to get, usually because it is very flexible, like a deep decision tree. For squared error there is an exact identity, made famous for machine learning by Geman, Bienenstock and Doursat: the expected test error equals the bias squared, plus the variance, plus the noise. Squared means multiplied by itself. Noise is the randomness in the outcome itself, which no model can predict. So the only choice we have is how to trade bias against variance: simpler models have more bias and less variance, flexible models the other way round. Part 1's single tree on slide 15 is the high variance case, and the forest that averages many trees is the standard way to cut variance.

Reference: Geman, Bienenstock, Doursat (1992), Neural Networks and the Bias/Variance Dilemma, Neural Computation 4(1), 1-58; Hastie et al. (2009), ESL, ch. 7

Full citation: Geman, S., Bienenstock, E. and Doursat, R. (1992). "Neural Networks and the Bias/Variance Dilemma." Neural Computation, 4(1), 1-58.

Full citation: Hastie, T., Tibshirani, R. and Friedman, J. (2009). The Elements of Statistical Learning (2nd ed.). Springer. Chapter 7 "Model Assessment and Selection".

## 31. Underfit, good fit, overfit

**Bonus minutes 9 to 10 (1 min).**

Here is the trade-off on a toy problem we generated ourselves: 30 points from a smooth wave, plus random noise. We fit three polynomials, curves whose flexibility is set by their degree, the highest power of x they use: degree 1 is a straight line, degree 4 can bend a few times, degree 15 can bend almost anywhere. The straight line underfits: it misses the wave, so it is poor on the training points and on new points alike. Its measured mean squared error is 0.229 on the training points and 0.311 on fresh points; the noise alone accounts for 0.09, the floor no curve can get below. Degree 4 fits the shape and ignores most of the noise: training error 0.063, test error 0.101, close to that floor. Degree 15 overfits: it chases individual noisy points, so its training error falls to 0.025 while its test error explodes to about 2,935, because between and beyond the training points the curve swings wildly. Every number on the panels was measured, by fitting on the 30 points and testing on 2,000 fresh points from the same wave; this is the pattern Part 1's noise experiment showed on real data.

Reference: James, Witten, Hastie, Tibshirani (2021), An Introduction to Statistical Learning, 2nd ed., Springer, ch. 2, Statistical Learning

Full citation: James, G., Witten, D., Hastie, T. and Tibshirani, R. (2021). An Introduction to Statistical Learning (2nd ed.). Springer. Chapter 2 "Statistical Learning".

## 32. The U-curve, in theory

**Bonus minutes 10 to 11 (1 min).**

This is the textbook U-curve, and it is computed, not sketched: we simulated many training sets, fitted polynomials of degree 1 to 12 to each, and measured the bias squared, the variance and the test error at every degree. Bias falls and variance rises as the degree grows, so the test error, their sum plus the noise, falls and then rises, with its lowest point in the middle, at degree 3. Bias plus variance plus noise matched the directly measured test error at every degree, which is the check that the decomposition holds. The next slide runs the same idea on our own stocks.

## 34. The noise floor

**Bonus minutes 12 to 13 (1 min).**

Remember the decomposition: test error is bias squared plus variance plus noise. In Part 1 the reference forest scored an R squared of plus 0.09 percent against predicting zero on the test years, which means its squared error was 99.91 percent of the zero forecast's. Almost all of the error was still there. We cannot split that leftover error into bias and noise exactly, but the research says most of it is noise: Gu, Kelly and Xiu found that even strong machine learning models explain under one percent of individual stock returns out of sample. When noise is the floor, there is little bias left to remove, so the extra flexibility of a complex model has almost nothing real to fit and fits the noise instead. That is why, in finance, controlling variance matters most, and why the next slide, regularisation, is the most useful tool in this section.

## 35. Regularisation

**Bonus minutes 13 to 14 (1 min).**

Regularisation is any brake on a model's flexibility. It deliberately adds a little bias in exchange for a large cut in variance, which is the right trade when noise dominates. For a linear model, a coefficient is the weight on one feature. Ridge regression, from Hoerl and Kennard, adds a penalty for large coefficients, so they all shrink towards zero as the penalty grows. The lasso, from Tibshirani, uses a slightly different penalty that pushes many coefficients to exactly zero, so it also chooses features. The chart is a toy problem we generated: 20 features, of which only 3 truly matter. As the penalty grows, ridge shrinks all twenty smoothly, while the lasso drops the useless ones to zero; in the measured run, at a penalty of 1.53 the lasso keeps exactly three coefficients, and they are the three true signals, while ridge sets none of the twenty exactly to zero at any penalty. Trees have their own brakes: a maximum depth, and a minimum number of training rows in each leaf. Part 1's reference forest used depth 6 and at least 50 rows per leaf, and the noise experiment showed what happens with the brakes off. Boosted trees and neural networks are trained step by step, so the standard brake is early stopping: watch the validation error and stop when it stops improving. And averaging, as a forest does, cancels much of the variance of its individual trees.

Reference: Hoerl, Kennard (1970), Ridge Regression, Technometrics 12(1), 55-67; Tibshirani (1996), J. Royal Statistical Society B 58(1), 267-288

Full citation: Hoerl, A. E. and Kennard, R. W. (1970). "Ridge Regression: Biased Estimation for Nonorthogonal Problems." Technometrics, 12(1), 55-67.

Full citation: Tibshirani, R. (1996). "Regression Shrinkage and Selection via the Lasso." Journal of the Royal Statistical Society: Series B, 58(1), 267-288.

## 36. Double descent (optional)

**Bonus minutes 14 to 15 (1 min).**

Optional slide: skip it when time is short; nothing later depends on it.

This is a modern twist on the U-curve, and it offers one explanation of why very large networks can still generalise. We fitted a linear model on randomly generated features of the input, on a toy problem with 100 training points, and varied the number of features from a handful to 2,000. With few features we see the usual U. As the number of features approaches the number of training points, written p equals n, the model can just fit every point exactly, noise included, and the test error spikes. Past that threshold there are many ways to fit the points exactly, and the method we used picks the smallest, smoothest one, called the minimum-norm solution. Test error then falls again: the second descent that Belkin, Hsu, Ma and Mandal described. Two cautions. The peak is real: at 100 features, exactly the number of points, the median test error was about 147, against 0.47 for the best small model with 20 features. And the second descent does not win here: the best of the very large models, at 1,400 features, reached about 0.85, still worse than the best small one. So say that error can fall again past the interpolation point, never that bigger models simply generalise better, and never skip validation because of it.

Reference: Belkin, Hsu, Ma, Mandal (2019), Reconciling modern machine-learning practice and the classical bias-variance trade-off, PNAS 116(32), 15849-15854

Full citation: Belkin, M., Hsu, D., Ma, S. and Mandal, S. (2019). "Reconciling modern machine-learning practice and the classical bias-variance trade-off." Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 116(32), 15849-15854.

## 37. The family map

**Bonus minutes 15 to 16 (1 min).**

Here are the seven families of models you will meet most often, each in one line. A linear model forecasts with a weighted sum of the features; a generalised linear model, a GLM, passes that sum through a link function, for example logistic regression, which turns it into a probability. k-nearest neighbours finds the k past examples most similar to today and averages what happened next. A decision tree asks yes or no questions, as in Part 1. Bagging fits many trees on resampled copies of the data and averages them; a random forest also gives each tree a random subset of the features. Boosting adds small trees one at a time, each one fitted to the errors left by the ones before. Kernel methods, including support vector machines, compare examples through a similarity function and then fit a simple boundary in that space. Neural networks stack layers of weighted sums with simple bends between them. The last card is a useful distinction from James and colleagues: a parametric model has a fixed number of parameters whatever the size of the data, while a non-parametric model can grow more detailed as data arrives. Part 1 compared the two marked families.

Reference: James, Witten, Hastie, Tibshirani (2021), An Introduction to Statistical Learning, 2nd ed., Springer, ch. 2, Statistical Learning

Full citation: James, G., Witten, D., Hastie, T. and Tibshirani, R. (2021). An Introduction to Statistical Learning (2nd ed.). Springer. Chapter 2 "Statistical Learning".

## 38. Model properties

**Bonus minutes 16 to 17 (1 min).**

This table is a rough guide, not a measurement: every cell is a judgement, and a particular dataset can overturn any of them. Flexibility is how many different shapes a model can fit. Interpretability is how easily a person can see why it made a forecast. Data needed is how many examples it takes before the model is reliable. Captures interactions asks whether it can learn that two features matter together, for example momentum only working when volatility is low, without being told. Extrapolates asks what happens outside the range of the training data: a linear model carries its straight line onwards, while a tree just repeats the value of its last leaf. Needs feature scaling asks whether the features must first be put on the same scale; trees do not care, because they only compare values, while distances and penalties do. Training cost is how much computing it takes. For noisy financial data, the columns that matter most are flexibility and data needed: the more flexible the model, the more data it needs before its variance is under control.

Reference: James et al. (2021), An Introduction to Statistical Learning, 2nd ed., ch. 2; Hastie et al. (2009), The Elements of Statistical Learning, 2nd ed.

## 39. Interpretability vs flexibility

**Bonus minutes 17 to 18 (1 min).**

This picture places the families on two axes: across, how flexible a model is; up, how easy it is to interpret. It follows a figure in James and colleagues' textbook, which places subset selection, the lasso, least squares, generalised additive models, trees, bagging and boosting, support vector machines and deep learning; we added ridge, k-nearest neighbours and random forests ourselves, and the chart marks those; ridge sits level with least squares, slightly to its right. ## 40. Explaining a model (optional)

**Bonus minutes 18 to 19 (1 min).**

Optional slide: skip it when time is short; nothing later depends on it.

Before trusting a model, ask what it relies on. There are four common ways to look. In a linear model, each coefficient is the change in the forecast for a one-unit change in that feature, holding the others fixed. For trees there are two kinds of feature importance, discussed in López de Prado's chapter 8. Impurity importance adds up how much each feature's splits improved the fit during training; it is fast, but it measures what was fitted, noise included. Permutation importance shuffles one feature on test data, breaking its link with the outcome, and measures how much worse the forecasts get; it measures what lasted. When we measured permutation importance on the workshop's reference forest with raw features, only ret_5, the one-week reversal feature, helped on the test years by more than one standard deviation; shuffling most of the others made the test error slightly smaller. SHAP values, from Lundberg and Lee, split each single forecast into a contribution from every feature, so you can explain one decision at a time. Rudin argues that for high-stakes decisions it is better to use a model that is interpretable by design than to explain a black box after the fact. Part 2 covers feature importance in code.

## 41. No free lunch

**Bonus minutes 19 to 20 (1 min).**

Wolpert proved a sobering result, nicknamed the no free lunch theorem: averaged over every possible problem, no learning algorithm does better than any other on examples it has not seen. It does not mean all models are equally good on your problem. It means that whenever a model wins, it wins because its built-in assumptions happen to match the structure of that problem, for example a forest assuming that the answer depends on yes or no questions about the features. So there is no best model in general, only a best model for a given kind of data, and the only way to find it is an honest comparison. Part 1 is a small example: on R squared against predicting zero, the constant forecast scored plus 0.73 percent, the linear model plus 0.45 percent and the forest plus 0.09 percent, so the simplest forecast won on that score. That is why every model in Part 1 was compared with a baseline first.

Reference: Wolpert (1996), The Lack of A Priori Distinctions Between Learning Algorithms, Neural Computation 8(7), 1341-1390

Full citation: Wolpert, D. H. (1996). "The Lack of A Priori Distinctions Between Learning Algorithms." Neural Computation, 8(7), 1341-1390.

## 42. Finance changes the rules

**Bonus minutes 20 to 22 (2 min).**

Everything in this section holds in finance, but the table from slide 3 changes how it applies. Three changes matter most. First, effective sample size: the number of truly independent observations. Our labels are five-day returns, so the label for Monday and the label for Tuesday share four of their five days. López de Prado measures this with average uniqueness: for each label, the average over its days of 1 divided by the number of labels covering that day; it is about 0.2 for 5-day labels. Computed for 1,000 five-day labels on a plain daily calendar it is 0.201, so 1,000 daily labels carry about the information of 201 independent ones, as the chart shows. Second, the curse of dimensionality, Bellman's phrase: every feature you add spreads the same data more thinly, so with few independent observations, many features make overfitting easier. Third, the metric must match the decision. In Part 1 the forest lost to a constant forecast on R squared, plus 0.09 percent against plus 0.73 percent, because the constant knew that shares rose on average. But a long/short book does not care about the average; it only cares which stocks beat the others on the same day. That is measured by the information coefficient, IC: the correlation between the forecasts and the outcomes across the stocks on one day. There the forest's mean daily IC was plus 0.011, above zero on 52.5 percent of days. That is small, and with overlapping labels it is not proof of skill, but it shows that the same forecasts can lose on one score and edge ahead on another. Choose the score that matches the trade you will make.

Reference: López de Prado (2018), Advances in Financial Machine Learning, ch. 4, Sample Weights; Bellman (1961), Adaptive Control Processes

Full citation: López de Prado, M. (2018). Advances in Financial Machine Learning. Wiley. Chapter 4 "Sample Weights".

Full citation: Bellman, R. (1961). Adaptive Control Processes: A Guided Tour. Princeton University Press.

## 43. Two cultures (optional)

**Bonus minutes 22 to 23 (1 min).**

Optional slide: skip it when time is short; nothing later depends on it.

Leo Breiman, who invented the random forest, wrote a famous essay about two ways of doing statistics. The data modelling culture assumes a simple model of how the data were produced, for example a straight line plus noise, estimates its parameters, and reads conclusions from them. Factor models in finance, which explain returns with a few common drivers, belong here. The algorithmic modelling culture treats the process as unknown and judges any method, a forest or a neural network, purely by how well it predicts new data. Breiman argued that statisticians relied too much on the first. Our own view, not Breiman's: finance needs both. Prediction picks the trades, but an economic reason why a signal works is what tells you when the regime has changed and the signal should no longer be trusted.

Reference: Breiman (2001), Statistical Modeling: The Two Cultures, Statistical Science 16(3), 199-231

Full citation: Breiman, L. (2001). "Statistical Modeling: The Two Cultures." Statistical Science, 16(3), 199-231.
